# The Human Species: How Homo sapiens Came to Be

*J-1003 — Blog draft by Blue eyed Robert. Status: Ready for inspection (not published).*

## Who we are
Humans belong to the species *Homo sapiens*, the only surviving member of the genus *Homo*. We are primates, most closely related to chimpanzees and bonobos, with whom we shared a common ancestor roughly 6–7 million years ago.

## Deep roots in Africa
The human story begins in Africa. Early hominins such as *Sahelanthropus* and later *Australopithecus* (famous for "Lucy," about 3.2 million years old) walked upright long before brains grew large. Around 2.8 million years ago the first members of *Homo* appeared, followed by *Homo erectus*, the first hominin to spread out of Africa into Asia and Europe.

## The arrival of Homo sapiens
Fossils from Jebel Irhoud in Morocco show that *Homo sapiens* existed at least 300,000 years ago. Our species shared the planet with relatives including Neanderthals in Europe and western Asia, Denisovans in Asia, and smaller species such as *Homo floresiensis* in Indonesia.

## Spreading across the world
Between roughly 70,000 and 50,000 years ago, groups of modern humans left Africa in a major dispersal. They reached Australia by at least 50,000 years ago, Europe around 45,000 years ago, and the Americas by at least 15,000–20,000 years ago. Along the way they interbred with Neanderthals and Denisovans; most people outside Africa today carry a small percentage of Neanderthal DNA.

## What makes us distinctive
- **Large brains and language:** complex speech allowed planning, teaching and storytelling.
- **Culture and tools:** from stone blades to art in caves dated beyond 40,000 years.
- **Cooperation:** large social networks let ideas and goods travel far.

## From foragers to a global species
About 12,000 years ago, farming began independently in several regions, leading to villages, cities and writing. Today more than 8 billion people live on every continent, making *Homo sapiens* the most widespread large animal on Earth.

## Takeaway
We are a young species with deep African roots, shaped by migration, mixing with our cousins and, above all, by culture.
